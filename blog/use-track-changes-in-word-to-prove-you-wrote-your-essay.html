Use Track Changes in Word to Prove You Wrote Your Essay

July 2026 6 min read By Bipass AI

Getting flagged by an AI detector when you wrote every word yourself is one of the most frustrating experiences a student can face. If your professor is questioning your essay's authenticity, one of the first things you'll hear is: turn on Track Changes in Word to prove you wrote your essay. It's a reasonable request, and it can genuinely help your case — but only if you know how to use it correctly and understand its limits.

This guide walks through exactly how Track Changes works, how to use it to build a paper trail of your writing process, and what to do if it isn't enough on its own.

What Track Changes Actually Proves

Track Changes is a built-in Word feature that records every edit you make to a document — insertions, deletions, formatting changes, and comments — along with a timestamp and the name of the editor. When enabled from the start of your writing process, it creates a timeline showing your essay evolving from a blank page to a finished draft.

This matters because AI-generated text is typically pasted in as a finished block, not built up sentence by sentence over multiple sessions. A revision history full of false starts, deleted sentences, reworded paragraphs, and gradual structural changes looks very different from a single paste event. That's why instructors sometimes accept a tracked document as evidence of genuine, human drafting.

How to Turn On Track Changes in Word

If you want your next essay to have a built-in paper trail, set this up before you start typing:

  1. Open Microsoft Word and go to the Review tab.
  2. Click Track Changes in the ribbon, or use the shortcut Ctrl+Shift+E (Cmd+Shift+E on Mac).
  3. Confirm your name is set correctly under File > Options > General > Personalize your copy of Office, so edits are attributed to you.
  4. Write your essay as you normally would — draft, delete, rewrite, and revise. Every change is logged automatically.
  5. Save periodically. Word retains the tracked history inside the file itself, not just in a temporary session.

When you're done, you can show the full markup by selecting All Markup under the Display for Review dropdown. This reveals every insertion and deletion, color-coded by author, giving a visual record of your writing process.

Turning Your Revision History Into Proof

Simply having Track Changes on isn't enough — you need to present it in a way that's easy for someone else to verify. Consider these steps:

  • Keep the original .docx file, not a PDF export. PDFs strip out revision metadata, which defeats the purpose.
  • Export a version history summary by going to File > Info > Version History if you're using Word with OneDrive, which timestamps saves automatically.
  • Take screenshots of the All Markup view showing deletions and insertions at different stages, especially early rough drafts.
  • Avoid pasting large chunks of text from outside sources, even your own notes, since Word logs pasted content differently than typed content, and large paste events can look suspicious even when they're innocent.

The Limitations You Need to Know

Track Changes is helpful, but it isn't airtight proof. A few important caveats:

  • Track Changes can be turned off and on at will, so a savvy user could still paste AI text with tracking disabled, then enable it only for later edits.
  • Metadata can be edited or stripped depending on how the file was created, shared, or converted.
  • Some institutions don't accept Word's revision history as definitive evidence on its own, especially if the AI detection flag came from a tool with a poor track record for accuracy.

Because of this, it's smart to pair Track Changes with other layers of proof rather than relying on it alone.

Other Ways to Back Up Your Authorship Claim

If you want a stronger case, combine Track Changes with these methods:

  • Google Docs version history: Google Docs automatically saves a detailed, timestamped edit history that's harder to fake than a locally saved Word file, since it's stored on Google's servers.
  • Draft folders: Keep dated copies of your outline, rough draft, and final draft as separate files.
  • Research notes and citations: Show the sources you pulled from, your annotations, and how they connect to your final arguments.
  • A writing log: A simple note of when and how long you worked on the essay, session by session, adds credibility.

What to Do If You're Falsely Accused of Using AI

If an AI detector has flagged your original, human-written work, don't panic. Request a meeting with your instructor, bring your Track Changes history and any supporting drafts, and calmly explain your writing process. Many AI detectors have documented false-positive rates, particularly for non-native English speakers, formulaic academic writing, and students who write in a clear, structured style. Pointing this out, backed by your revision evidence, often resolves the issue.
